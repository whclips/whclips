Automation Stack · pit-to-cloud data for mining

One trusted picture of the mine, right now.

White Horse Clips builds the layer that the mine's existing systems never had: a single, live, semantically organised source of truth, and the map of meaning on top of it that lets people and agents answer is this asset making money right now, and if not, exactly why.

The core problem we solve

What is it, in one sentence, and who feels it?

The mine is run on the average, not the moment. Every system holds a slice of the truth, none of them talk, and by the time the numbers are reconciled the shift that lost the money is over.

Stated plainly, this is what we are hired to fix:

Last month's cost per tonne arrives two weeks after close. Last shift's OEE arrives on a printed report. The alarm on section three arrives at the morning stand-up. So nobody can say whether this operation is making money right now — and because nobody can say that, nobody can say why. You are left managing the average instead of the moment.The problem in one paragraph, and the reason every one of these systems was bought in the first place

What it costs

One haul truck stood down takes a third of that face's hauling with it: the excavator waits, the stockpile drops, the plant runs short. Our demo computes exactly that, live — capacity, tonnes and rand for every idle hour. The cost was never invisible. It was spread across four systems that do not speak to each other.

Why nobody has fixed it

The answer needs PLC physics, SCADA exceptions, MES plan-versus-actual and ERP rates combined in real time. That takes full-stack fluency almost nobody has, access an IT department rarely grants, and an architecture the next question can build on.

What we sell

Not a dashboard. The single real-time source of truth at asset level, calculated once and published where every screen, report and agent can read it without understanding the five systems underneath.

The Unified Namespace

What is it, and why is it what we build first?

The structure of the business and every event in it, organised the way the business is organised, holding the current state of everything, as the single source of truth for right now.

How it is built

Walk the value stream. Connect the PLCs and the databases. Organise the business on ISA-95 (enterprise, site, area, line, cell). Bring in the raw points and abstract them to current state, one payload shape: value, timestamp, quality, units.

Why it scales

Edge-driven, report by exception, lightweight, open. 93% of points do not change inside a minute, so only changes travel. New use cases extend the tree; nothing is rebuilt.

What it is not

Not the MQTT broker. The broker is the how; the namespace is the what. And not a one-way "digital thread": every smart thing is a node that both publishes and consumes, so the PLC can read the OEE it helped produce.

We already have one. Our live demo environment joins Mosquitto and MQTT Engine, Sepasoft MES, Ignition, the Atlas CMMS and a Sage VIP presence feed into one live operating picture. It is a Unified Namespace and we have never called it that on the site.

The knowledge graph

What the namespace is the foundation for.

The namespace says where data lives. The graph says how the plant behaves: what feeds what, what gates what, which two views are the same asset. Meaning, without a human reasoning it.
  • Sequence is settled. Start with the namespace, then extend it into the graph so that as one grows the other does. Nobody has managed to start with the graph.
  • It is the scaffold agents traverse. A language model is context-limited; the graph walks it to the node that matters, then it reads current state there. Without a graph there is no real agentic reasoning across a plant.
  • It makes tribal knowledge queryable. What an experienced operator says to an agent is applied to the graph and answers the next operator's question. Root cause traces across silos; asset types compare across sites.
  • The tooling has just arrived, and it is in our toolset. Flow Software's Timebase: a free-forever historian with no tag or user limits, collectors for MQTT, Sparkplug B and OPC UA, and Atlas, a purpose-built, git-versioned, agent-native knowledge graph that ships with Timebase 2.0 in September 2026 (release candidates today). Founded in South Africa, now in Austin, with PPC Cement and Coca-Cola Beverages Africa among its South African references. Stood up from nothing in four and a half hours on a live stream. Two cautions from that stream: the agent inferred bindings wrong 11 times in 24 and flagged only two, and component secrets will drive the support load.

How we make money

Three revenue lines, each one earning the right to the next.

Nothing here is sold on a promise. Every stage is paid, leaves the client an artefact they keep, and has to justify the stage after it.

The front door is a paid assessment, not a proposal. Two weeks, fixed price, on site. We inventory the operation and its intelligence, walk the value stream end to end, and rate every data source for whether it can actually be trusted — in sessions with operations, maintenance, engineering, OT and leadership. The client ends with three things they cannot buy anywhere else: a digital strategy short enough for every employee to recite, an architecture in which every smart thing both publishes and consumes, and the minimum technical requirements any future vendor must meet to join it. It is deliberately priced to be approved by a manager rather than a board, which is what makes it a door rather than a deal.

Then a fixed-price, twelve-week proof on a single asset. One asset, one question: is this asset making money right now, and if not, exactly why. We build the namespace beneath it and join the four systems that between them hold the answer. The client ends with one number they did not have before — what one hour of that asset stopped actually costs, measured on their own plant rather than quoted from someone else's. That number decides whether there is a second iteration, and it is deliberately a number we cannot manufacture.

The recurring line is a per-site subscription for the namespace and the decision layer. It starts once the proof lands and grows as the tree extends — one machine, then the fleet, then downstream, then the next area — because the namespace was built to be extended rather than replaced. Each iteration also hands more of the routine work to the client's own people: we do all of the first, then ninety, eighty, seventy, sixty percent, teaching them the ordinary work while we take the harder problems. That sounds like revenue given away and it is the opposite. It is what makes the subscription defensible, because the client stays for the layer and the judgment rather than for the labour.

Where we play: Industry 4 and Industry 5

What the next two years look like, and where White Horse Clips sits in them.

Industry 3 automated the equipment. Industry 4 automates the decision. Industry 5 is human judgment and machine reasoning working together — and it can only run on the infrastructure Industry 4 builds.

Industry 3

The automation of industrial processes. PLCs, HMIs, drives, safety interlocks. It still matters and it is not going away, but it has become a commodity. We do not compete here.

Industry 4 · where we build

The automation of business decisions, on a common digital infrastructure that shows the current state of the operation. This is where the Unified Namespace comes from, and it consumes the data Industry 3 already generates. This is our foundation work.

Industry 5 · where we are going

The convergence of human and artificial intelligence to make better decisions, running on the Industry 4 infrastructure. Most firms selling this today have never run a plant. We intend to be the ones who can do both.

The line we will not cross

A PLC is deterministic: the same input gives the same output, every time, inside a guaranteed window. That is the whole basis of industrial control, and it is not a candidate for probabilistic reasoning. A language model is probabilistic by design — the same prompt and the same context can still take a different path. You can constrain it, and a human in the loop is the best constraint there is, but you cannot make it deterministic.

So agents live in the decision layer, not the control layer. Our rule of thumb: if a wrong or late answer can hurt someone or break something, it stays deterministic. If a wrong answer that gets caught costs a review cycle, that is where agents earn their place. Anyone who blurs that line in a mine should not be trusted with it.

What you buy, and what gets built

The honest split, and the one we will advise you on rather than sell around. Buy the infrastructure: brokers, historians, modelling engines, data transformation, the pipelines. That market is mature and building your own is waste. Build what sits above it: how your people and your agents analyse, visualise, find patterns, report and solve. That is where your operation is different from the one next door, and it is the part no vendor can sell you off a shelf.

What full-stack fluency now means

  • Model layer — large and small language models, context management, and the cost per turn of running them. Token economics belongs in every decision now.
  • Orchestration — MCP, tool calling, multi-agent workflows, guardrails, human in the loop.
  • Data — contextualisation, semantic modelling, the namespace as the substrate, and knowledge graphs for traversing relationships.
  • Engineering — agentic DevOps, evaluation, observability, security and permissions.
  • Judgment — knowing when to build and when to buy, and being able to take a vendor's AI claim apart. Most of them do not survive it.

The problems themselves have not changed: do more with less, do it safer, do it with less impact, get more out of what you already own. Those are data-to-decision problems and they always were. What is changing, fast, is the mechanism for solving them.

The operator, and the ask

Who is building this, what has he already delivered, and what does he need?

Twenty-one years inside mining operations, not selling into them. The domain knowledge and the architecture are not the gap. The software team and the capital are.

Johan Coenraad du Preez has spent twenty-one years in mining, all of it operational. Most recently he was OT & Automation Manager at Tharisa Minerals, a PGM and chrome co-producer, owning a department of more than forty-eight people across three open-pit mines and three processing plants — optimisation, data analytics, monitor and control, and OT governance including the cybersecurity framework and the enterprise data platform. Before that came nearly nine years at Xstrata Alloys, now Glencore, ending as Maintenance Superintendent of a platinum concentrator, leading a thirty-four-person mechanical, electrical and instrumentation team and implementing ISO 55000 asset management across crushers, mills, flotation and 33kV substations. He serves as Deputy Chair of the RTIMS Technical Steering Committee at the Mandela Mining Precinct — a public-private partnership between the Department of Science and Innovation and the Minerals Council of South Africa — leading the IT-OT convergence work package and contributing to the AI framework for mining.

Two results are worth stating because they were measured on a plant rather than modelled on a slide. He directed a neural-network haulage digital twin and introduced volumetric scanning, together delivering a 17% load improvement and more than $60M in annual revenue impact. And the automated load-curtailment response he directed — the plant answering a grid curtailment event on its own, protecting production through constrained supply while cutting two thousand kilograms of CO2 per hour of runtime — won the Schneider Electric 2024 Sustainability Impact Award. He directed both as the department head who carried the budget, the team and the accountability for them.

Where this stands today: pre-revenue. The demo described in this document is built and running on production hardware, and the first turnkey proposal is in front of a coal producer's executive team. An operator who has actually run the plant, paired with builders who ship software, is the combination this needs to become a product company rather than a consultancy.
